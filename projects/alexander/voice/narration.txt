In three fifty-six B.C., a baby boy was born in Pella, the capital of Macedonia, a rugged mountain kingdom on the northern edge of the Greek world. His name was Alexander. Before he turned thirty, he would rule one of the largest empires the ancient world had ever seen. And he would never lose a major battle.

His father was King Philip the Second, a tough soldier-king who had lost an eye in battle, and turned Macedonia's farmers into the most dangerous army in Greece. According to Plutarch, when Alexander was a boy, nobody could tame a wild horse called Bucephalus. Alexander noticed the horse was afraid of its own shadow, so he turned it to face the sun, and rode it. Philip decided a son like that needed the best teacher money could buy. So he hired the philosopher Aristotle. At a quiet school in Mieza, Aristotle taught the thirteen-year-old prince philosophy, mathematics, science and medicine, and how a leader should think. Alexander loved Homer's Iliad so much that he kept a copy under his pillow... right next to his dagger. His father's generals taught him the other subject: war. At just eighteen, Alexander led the cavalry charge at the Battle of Chaeronea, and helped his father take control of Greece.

Then, in three thirty-six B.C., everything changed. At a royal wedding in the old capital, Aegae, King Philip walked into the theatre... and was assassinated by one of his own bodyguards. Alexander was about twenty years old. Overnight, he was king. Some Greek cities thought the boy-king would be easy to push around. They were wrong. He crushed the revolts, and turned to face the largest empire on Earth.

The Persian Empire stretched from Egypt to India, and its king, Darius the Third, could raise armies far bigger than Alexander's. In three thirty-four B.C., Alexander crossed into Asia with roughly forty thousand men. Legend says he threw a spear into the beach as he landed, claiming the whole continent. In three thirty-three B.C., at Issus, the two kings finally met. Darius had the bigger army, but the battlefield was squeezed between the mountains and the sea, so his numbers didn't help. Alexander charged straight at Darius... and Darius turned his chariot around and fled. Darius offered him a huge piece of his empire for peace. His general Parmenion said, "If I were Alexander, I'd take it." Alexander replied, "So would I, if I were Parmenion." In three thirty-one B.C., they met again at Gaugamela, on a wide flat plain chosen so Darius's huge army and scythed chariots could spread out. Alexander's soldiers simply opened gaps in their lines, and let the chariots roll right through. Then Alexander found a weak spot, charged... and Darius fled. Again.

Babylon, Susa and Persepolis fell, and Alexander was now the king of Asia. But he didn't stop. His empire already reached from Greece to Egypt, where he was welcomed as pharaoh. Now it pushed across Persia, into the mountains of Bactria and Sogdiana, in today's Afghanistan and Uzbekistan, and in three twenty-six B.C., across the Indus into northwestern India, where he beat King Porus and his war elephants at the Hydaspes River. There, after eight years of marching, his exhausted army refused to go any farther. So, for once, Alexander turned back.

Along the way, he founded cities, and named many of them after himself. The most famous was in Egypt. Earlier, in three thirty-one B.C., Alexander marked out a new city on the Mediterranean coast. Legend says the builders outlined its streets with barley flour, and when birds flew down and ate it, the seers said the city would one day feed the world. Alexandria grew into one of the greatest cities on Earth, famous for its lighthouse and its library, both built by the kings who came after him.

Alexander never saw it grow. In June, three twenty-three B.C., in Babylon, he fell ill with a fever. Days later, Alexander the Great was dead, at just thirty-two years old. To this day, nobody knows for sure what killed him.

He left no clear heir. Asked who should rule after him, he is said to have answered: "The strongest." His generals fought over his empire for decades, until it broke apart into separate kingdoms: the Ptolemies in Egypt, the Seleucids in Asia, and the Antigonids in Macedonia. But his real legacy wasn't the land. Greek language, art and ideas spread from Egypt to the edge of India, blending with the cultures they met, and began an era historians call the Hellenistic Age. He ruled for just thirteen years. People have been talking about him for more than two thousand years.
